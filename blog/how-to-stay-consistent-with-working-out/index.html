How to stay consistent with working out when you keep quitting

The easiest way to stay consistent with working out is to start small and let something else remind you. Most people don't quit because they lack discipline. They quit because the plan was too big to survive a bad week, and one missed workout made it feel like the whole thing was ruined. A 7-year study of 587 people in group exercise programs found the biggest predictors of quitting were things like their goals, not their personality [1]. So pick something you can do on your worst day, and if you miss one, pick it back up tomorrow.

Why workout plans stop working

Every plan is written by you on a good day. You're motivated, you've got a free Sunday, and you map out 4 workouts a week at 6am. Then the plan gets handed to a different you, the one who slept badly and has a 7:30 meeting.

The plan assumes that future you will be available, rested, and willing at the exact time you picked. Some weeks that's true. Plenty of weeks it isn't.

Which part of a plan does the work?

Here's every job a workout plan is supposed to do, and which ones tend to break.

Notice the top two rows. Knowing what to do is almost never the problem. You can find a hundred free workouts in 5 minutes.

In that 7-year study, everyone was in a supervised group [1]. Somebody expected them to show up. That's why the same person can go to a class for years but can't make themselves work out at home. The exercise at home is easier. Getting started isn't.

Why missing one workout ends the whole thing

Most plans only have two states. You're on the plan, or you're off it. There's nothing in between.

So when you miss Tuesday, it doesn't feel like you missed 25% of your week. It feels like you broke the plan. And once it's broken, there's not much reason to do Wednesday.

Any system where one miss wipes out the attempt is eventually going to get wiped out. The fix is to make the thing you can fail at really small. If missing costs you one 90-second challenge instead of the whole plan, there's no reason to quit over it.

How to stay consistent with working out

Here's what I'd do instead of writing another plan.

The first is to make it small enough to survive a bad day. If it only works when you've got an hour free, it won't last. A few minutes is plenty to start. In one big study, people who got about 4.4 minutes a day of short, hard bursts had much lower risk of dying early than people who got none [2].

The second is to get the reminder out of your own head. The coach and the group in that study were doing this job. You need something that taps you on the shoulder, whether that's a friend, a class, or an app.

The third is to pick a goal that tells you right away whether you did it. "Lose 20 pounds" takes months to answer. "Do today's push-ups" is answered by tonight.

The fourth is to make a miss cheap. One missed day should cost one day.

And the last one is to stop scheduling against your worst hours. If you're wrecked at 6pm, don't plan your workouts for 6pm.

Frequently asked questions

Is it normal to quit a workout plan?

Very normal, and it doesn't mean you're bad at this. Plans assume your week will go the way you pictured it, and most weeks don't. The good news is people can stick with exercise long term. In one 7-year study, over half the people in group programs were still going after a year.

How do I get motivated to work out when I have no motivation?

Don't wait for motivation. It comes and goes, and you can't plan around it. Make the first step so small it doesn't need motivation, like 10 squats, and let something outside your head remind you. Once you're moving, the motivation tends to show up on its own.

Does having a weight-loss goal make me more likely to quit?

In one big study of group exercise programs, people with weight-loss goals did tend to drop out sooner. It's not that the goal is bad. Weight changes slowly, so it's easy to feel like nothing's working. Pair it with a goal you can check off today, like finishing your challenges.

Should I just try harder?

I'd try smaller instead. Trying harder usually means a bigger plan, and a bigger plan has more ways to break. A tiny habit you do every day beats a big one you do for 3 weeks. You can always add more once the small version is automatic.

What's the minimum amount of exercise worth doing?

One large study found that adults who did no formal exercise but got about 3 short bursts of hard effort a day, around 4.4 minutes total, had 38-40% lower risk of dying from any cause. It's observational, but it's a good reason not to wait for an hour.
